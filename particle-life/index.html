Six species. One interaction matrix. Each species has a force toward every other — attraction at medium range, repulsion up close, nothing at distance. The forces are pairwise only. No cell knows it's a cell. No predator knows it's hunting. No chain knows it's a chain.

And yet: cells with membranes form. Chains self-assemble. One species chases another across the field. Crystalline lattices grow and shatter. The structures look alive — they move, they breathe, they eat each other — and they are built from nothing but a table of numbers that says "red likes blue, blue hates green, green tolerates yellow."

This is Particle Life, discovered by Tom Schmitz in 2009 and refined by Jeffrey Ventrella and others. It answers the Forge question from a new angle: the order isn't in the particles, it's in the relationship matrix. The genome is the geometry. Change one sign in the matrix and the entire ecosystem restructures — a stable cell dissolves, a new predator emerges, a crystalline civilization collapses into soup.

Click the simulation to add particles. Press New Genome for a fresh random interaction matrix — every genome is a different world. Expand the matrix to see and edit the forces directly. Some genomes are dead — everything clumps. Some are frantic — everything scatters. The interesting ones are in between, and you'll know them when you see them.

The mechanism

Each particle has a species (color). For every pair of species (i, j), the matrix defines a force F(i,j). At close range, all particles repel (hard core). At medium range, F(i,j) determines attraction (positive) or repulsion (negative). Beyond a cutoff, no interaction. Particles have friction. That's the entire system — no collision detection, no energy, no rules about what should form. The structures are emergent: they exist in the dynamics, not in the rules. The same way a flock exists in the boids' steering forces, not in any bird's intention to flock. The matrix is the DNA; the simulation is the phenotype.
