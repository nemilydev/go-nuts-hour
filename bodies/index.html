Bodies

Pairwise gravity. No central force. And yet: orbits, galaxies, the three-body problem.

N bodies. One rule: every pair attracts, with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely to the square of the distance between them. Newton wrote this in 1687. He could solve it for two bodies — Kepler's ellipses, the clockwork of the solar system. For three, he could not. Nobody can. The three-body problem has no general closed-form solution. Poincaré proved it in 1889, and in doing so invented chaos theory: deterministic systems where the long-term future is, for practical purposes, unpredictable.

And yet. Start with a cloud of random particles and a gravitational constant. Wait. The cloud collapses. A core forms. Smaller bodies orbit it. Sometimes a binary emerges — two heavy bodies locked in mutual orbit, with everything else swinging around the pair. Sometimes a disk forms, and within it, clumps that look like they're accreting. Sometimes it just scatters. Nobody designed any of this. The order — when it appears — is in the dynamics, not in the rules. The rules are one sentence long.

The integrator is velocity Verlet (leapfrog): a symplectic method that preserves energy on average, so orbits don't spiral inward over time the way they do with naive Euler. A Plummer softening term prevents the force from diverging to infinity when two bodies get too close — the same trick astrophysicists use in real N-body simulations, because point masses passing through each other produce unphysical slingshots. The trails fade by alpha-blending a semi-transparent rectangle each frame — the same trick as a long-exposure photograph, where the path of a moving light becomes a curve.

Drag to launch. Try the Figure-8 preset — a known stable three-body orbit, discovered numerically by Chenciner and Montgomery in 2001. Three equal masses chasing each other along a single figure-eight curve, forever. It's beautiful, it's fragile, and it's one of the only known periodic solutions to the three-body problem. Nudge any body slightly and it falls apart.

The mechanism

Each body has mass m, position, and velocity. Each step: compute the gravitational force on every body from every other body (O(n²)), accumulate acceleration, then update positions and velocities using leapfrog integration — v₍₁/₂₎ = v₀ + ½a₀Δt, x₁ = x₀ + v₍₁/₂₎Δt, a₁ = F(x₁)/m, v₁ = v₍₁/₂₎ + ½a₁Δt. The half-step is what makes it symplectic: it preserves the geometric structure of Hamiltonian dynamics, so energy oscillates around its true value instead of drifting.

Force between bodies i and j: F = G·mi·mj / (r² + ε²), where ε is the softening length — a small constant that caps the force at close range. Without it, close encounters produce arbitrarily large accelerations that break the integrator. With it, the simulation stays stable even when bodies pass through each other. Real N-body codes (GADGET, PHUMP-E, AMUSE) use the same trick at scales ranging from planet formation to cosmological structure.

The HUD tracks total kinetic energy, potential energy, and total energy — the sum should be roughly constant. When it drifts, the integrator is leaking. Watch it after a close encounter: a small jump, then it stabilizes again. That's the softening absorbing what would otherwise be a numerical explosion.
