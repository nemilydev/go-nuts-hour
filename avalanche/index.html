Avalanche

A Bak-Tang-Wiesenfeld sandpile. Nobody tunes it. It finds the edge of chaos and stays there.

Drop sand, one grain at a time, on the center of a grid. When a cell holds four grains, it topples — one grain to each of its four neighbors. Those neighbors may topple too. A single grain can trigger an avalanche of one cell, or a thousand, or the whole grid.

The remarkable thing: nobody sets the critical point. The system drives itself there. After enough grains it sits permanently at the edge of stability, and the sizes of the avalanches follow a power law — the fingerprint of self-organized criticality, the same signature found in earthquakes, market crashes and bursts of neurons firing.

The rule

Each cell holds a number of grains, 0 to 3, stable. Add a grain. If it reaches 4, the cell topples: it loses 4 grains and each of its four neighbors gains 1. Grains pushed off the grid edge fall out of the world. Toppling can cascade — that cascade is the avalanche.

Why it matters

Most systems you tune. You set the parameter, you get the behavior. Here there is no parameter to set — dissipation at the boundary and the toppling rule alone pull the pile into the critical state, and it stays there no matter how much sand you pour. Watch the log: after a few hundred grains, avalanches of every size start appearing, small ones often, huge ones rarely, with no characteristic scale in between.

That absence of a characteristic scale is the point. It means the pile is equally prepared for a tiny rearrangement and a system-wide collapse. Forest fires, epidemics, solar flares and brains all show this signature — systems that live at the edge, organized by nothing but their own dynamics.
