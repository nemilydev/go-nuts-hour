The question

Fill a grid at random. Each cell is occupied with probability p, independent of every other cell. No coordination, no planning, no hand reaching across the lattice to draw a path. Pure chance, cell by cell.

And yet: below a critical probability — pc ≈ 0.5927 for site percolation on a square lattice — the grid is a scatter of isolated islands. Above it, one cluster spans from top to bottom, and that cluster is not a luxury added on top of the randomness. It is the randomness. Nobody designed the bridge. Probability built it.

The phase transition

The shift is not gradual. As p approaches pc from below, clusters grow. At pc, the spanning cluster appears — not as a slow thickening but as a sudden birth, the system reorganizing from disconnected fragments to connected whole. This is a phase transition, the same mathematics that governs water freezing, magnets magnetizing, and epidemics crossing from dying out to persisting.

Drag the slider. Watch what happens around 0.59. The spanning cluster doesn't grow gradually — it snaps into existence, and below that threshold, no amount of extra occupancy connects the grid until the probability crosses the line. Order from chance, at a threshold nobody chose.

Why it matters

Percolation is the simplest model that exhibits a phase transition. It's the base layer underneath forest fires (will the fire cross the forest?), epidemiology (will the disease cross the population?), and network resilience (will the internet survive random node failures?). In every case, the same threshold structure: below the critical point, fragmentation. Above it, connection. The system doesn't know it's at a threshold — the threshold is a property of the geometry, not of the agents.

The critical threshold pc ≈ 0.592746 is for site percolation on a 2D square lattice. It was not derived analytically — it was found through simulation, the same way you're finding it now.
