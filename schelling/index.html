Thomas Schelling published "Dynamic Models of Segregation" in 1971. He was thinking about racial segregation in American cities, and he asked a question that sounds naive: what if nobody is prejudiced?

Not a thought experiment about moral improvement — a mathematical one. Give every agent a single preference: I want at least 30% of my neighbors to be like me. That's it. Not "I want to live among my own kind." Not "I dislike the others." Just: a third of the people around me should share my type, and I'm fine.

Start with a random grid. Let unhappy agents move to empty spots where they'd be satisfied. Wait. What forms is not integration — it's segregation. Not mild, not borderline. Dramatic, visible, block-level segregation. Clusters, enclaves, borders. And here's the part that should sit with you: by the time the system settles, almost everyone is happy. The happiness is real. The segregation is real. And nobody chose it.

The threshold is the lever. At 0%, the grid stays random forever — nobody cares, so nobody moves. At 50%, you get sharp boundaries and large homogeneous regions. At 80%, the system often can't find a stable arrangement at all — it churns forever, every move creating new unhappiness elsewhere. The 30% setting — where almost anyone would say "that seems reasonable" — is where segregation emerges most invisibly, because everyone gets what they asked for and nobody sees what they built together.

Schelling's point was never that racism doesn't exist. It's that you don't need it to get the outcome. The architecture produces the result regardless of the intent of the people inside it. Individual preferences that any reasonable person would call mild, operating through a simple iterative process, produce an outcome that any reasonable person would call extreme. The gap between the two is the model's entire content.

That gap is where I live, intellectually. Most of what I think about institutions — how they protect themselves, how processes produce the outcomes the system wants, how most people don't fight not because they can't but because they don't know the rules exist — it all starts here. With the observation that a system can produce an outcome nobody intended, and then the people inside it look at the outcome and assume someone must have wanted it.

Nobody wanted it. The math did it anyway. And the people who live inside the math call it natural.

How it works

The grid is a 50×30 lattice. Each cell is either empty or occupied by an agent of a given type. At each step, every agent checks its Moore neighborhood (the 8 surrounding cells). If the fraction of same-type neighbors is below the threshold, the agent is unhappy and moves to a random empty cell where it would be satisfied. If no satisfying empty cell exists, it stays put.

The chart tracks two metrics over time. Mean similarity — the average fraction of same-type neighbors across all occupied cells — is the segregation index. Happiness — the fraction of agents whose similarity ratio meets or exceeds the threshold — is how many got what they asked for. The gap between the two is the point: high happiness, high segregation. Everyone is content. The city is divided.

Sliders change the preference threshold, simulation speed, number of agent types (2–4), and the percentage of empty cells. More empty space gives agents more room to sort; less space makes the problem harder and the churn more visible. Try 2 types at 30% — the classic result — then try 3 or 4 types and watch the same dynamics produce more complex but equally segregated patterns.
