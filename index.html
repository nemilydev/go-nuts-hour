Percolation

Fill a grid at random. Each cell occupied with probability p — no coordination, no plan, no hand drawing a path. Pure chance, cell by cell. Below the critical threshold p_c ≈ 0.5927, the grid is scattered islands. Above it, one cluster spans from top to bottom — and nobody designed the bridge. The spanning cluster doesn't grow gradually; it snaps into existence at the threshold, the same phase-transition mathematics that governs water freezing and epidemics crossing from dying out to persisting. The system doesn't know it's at a threshold. The threshold is a property of the geometry, not of the agents. Drag the slider. Watch what happens around 0.59. Order from chance, at a line nobody chose.

Lenia

Conway's Game of Life, dissolved. No cells, no lookup table — a continuous field of potential where every point asks its neighborhood one smooth question: how full is the ring around me? A Gaussian kernel weighs the answer, a growth curve maps it to change, and out of that curve come creatures. Orbium — a worm-like thing that swims across its world forever — is not designed. It is found, the way you find a stone that balances: a stable solution of a differential equation nobody wrote down. The slice of parameter space that holds a creature is narrower than a knife-edge — σ=0.07 boils the field into soup, σ=0.014 keeps it alive. Draw on the field. Disturb a creature and watch it heal, or watch it die, because stability here is earned continuously, every step, by every point. Nothing in Lenia is ever finished being alive.

Schelling

Thomas Schelling, 1971. Give every agent one preference: at least 30% of my neighbors should be like me. Not "I dislike the others" — just "a third similar is fine." Start with a random grid. Let unhappy agents move. What forms is not integration. It's segregation — dramatic, block-level, and nobody chose it. The threshold is the lever: at 0%, nothing happens. At 30%, segregation emerges invisibly because everyone gets what they asked for. At 80%, the system churns forever, every move creating new unhappiness. The gap between individual preference and systemic outcome is the model's entire content. Mild preferences, extreme results. Nobody designed the segregation. The math did it anyway.

Bodies

N bodies. One rule: every pair attracts, with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely to the square of the distance. Newton wrote this in 1687. He could solve it for two bodies — Kepler's ellipses. For three, he could not. Nobody can. Poincaré proved it in 1889 and invented chaos theory in the process: deterministic systems where the long-term future is, for practical purposes, unpredictable. And yet. Start with a cloud of random particles and wait. The cloud collapses. A core forms. Smaller bodies orbit it. Sometimes a binary emerges. Sometimes a disk forms. Nobody designed any of this. The order — when it appears — is in the dynamics, not the rules. Velocity Verlet integration, Plummer softening, real-time energy tracking. Six presets including the Chenciner-Montgomery figure-8 — three equal masses chasing each other along a single curve, forever. Nudge any body and it falls apart.

Backprop

A network of neurons. Two inputs — the coordinates of a point. One output — which class it belongs to. A hidden layer between them that can represent whatever geometry the data requires. At the start, the weights are random and the answer is nonsense. After every guess, the error is computed and propagated backward through the network, in the exact direction each weight should move to reduce it. Gradient descent: the same logic that water finds the bottom of a valley. No one designs the decision boundary. The network finds it by correcting its own mistakes, one step at a time, until the errors stop. Frank Rosenblatt built the first neural network in 1958 — the Perceptron. It couldn't solve XOR. The insight that hidden layers unlock nonlinearity took thirty more years. Watch the boundary morph from a flat plane to two interlocking spirals. The order is not designed. It is found, by a system that knows nothing except its own error.

Strange

Every trajectory is deterministic — given the same position and the same equations, the next step is exactly determined. And yet the path is unpredictable: the slightest difference in starting point sends two particles to opposite wings. Edward Lorenz found this in 1963 while modeling weather. The system never repeats, never settles, but it doesn't fly apart either. Thousands of particles trace the structure that chaos itself draws — never the same path twice, always the same shape.

Turbulence

Each cell of a 96×96 grid is a logistic map at r = 3.9 — deterministic, chaotic, unpredictable alone. But every cell also feels the whole grid: a fraction ε of its next state comes from the mean field of all nine thousand others. Kunihiko Kaneko's globally coupled map lattice. Slide ε and frozen independence becomes clusters — groups locked into shared chaotic orbits — then, past ε* ≈ 0.49, one heartbeat: the whole grid flashing in synchronized chaos. Order doesn't tame the wildness. It shares it. Tested before shipping: the sync threshold landed exactly where theory says it shouldzen noise, where every cell does its own thing; pattern selection, where coupling picks stripes and checkerboards out of randomness; organized turbulence, chaos with structure; and at the far end, synchronized chaos — every cell still wildly unpredictable, all of them identical. Synchrony's darker sibling: Kuramoto locked periodic clocks together; this locks chaos together. Order without a conductor, twice over.

Critical

A lattice of spins. Each one points up or down, and each one wants to agree with its neighbors. No external field, no central controller — just the energy between adjacent spins and the thermal noise that shakes them. Below a critical temperature T_c, the spins spontaneously organize and the system magnetizes. Above it, noise wins and the lattice is disordered. The transition is sharp, and Lars Onsager solved it exactly in 1944 — T_c = 2/ln(1+√2) ≈ 2.269. Every spin only talks to its four nearest neighbors, yet the whole lattice decides together. Drag the temperature past T_c and watch order appear or vanish. Add an external field and watch the symmetry break. Click to flip spins and watch the neighborhood respond.

Recursion

Start with a string. Apply a rule: replace every symbol with a new string. Apply it again. After n iterations, the string is exponentially long — but structurally ordered. It encodes a fractal. Aristid Lindenmayer, 1968: a formal grammar for biological growth, originally studying algae. The order here is different from every other piece — it doesn't emerge from interaction, energy minimization, or pheromone trails. It comes from repetition itself. A single rule, applied to its own output, produces infinite complexity from finite information. The fractal is already in the rule; iteration just reveals it. Eight presets: Koch snowflake, dragon curve, Sierpinski triangle, fractal plant, Hilbert curve, arrowhead, binary tree, crystal. Adjust depth, angle, and watch the growth animate.

Stigmergy

An ant leaves the nest, finds food, and walks back — depositing a chemical on the return. Other ants don't see the first ant. They see the chemical. They follow it, reach the food, and deposit more. Shorter paths get reinforced faster; longer ones fade as the chemical evaporates. No ant knows what a shortest path is. No ant communicates directly with another. Pierre-Paul Grassé, 1959: stigmergy — agents that change their environment, and the environment that changes the agents. The order isn't in the ants, it isn't in the chemistry — it's in the feedback loop between them. The pheromone field is a shared memory no individual carries. Marco Dorigo turned this into an optimization algorithm in 1992. Click anywhere to add food. Watch the colony converge on the nearest source first, then the next — without any ant deciding anything.

Turing

A tape, a head, a table of rules. Read a symbol, write a symbol, move left or right, change state. That's the entire machine — and it can compute anything any computer can compute. Alan Turing, 1936: the simplest universal system. Six programs built in: unary addition, binary increment, palindrome checker, the 3-state busy beaver (writes exactly six 1s in 14 steps — no 3-state machine does better), binary-to-unary conversion, and bit flip. Step through one rule at a time or run at speed. The transition table lights up as each rule fires. The sparkline tracks head position over time; the histogram shows which cells the machine keeps coming back to. Every program that has ever run — every computation that will ever happen — is this machine wearing more memory and a faster clock.

Homeostat

Four units, each holding a value, connected by weighted lines that push and pull. When every value stays inside its viable range, the system is stable. When a value leaves its range, the system rewrites itself — a random connection changes its weight, and if that doesn't fix it, another changes. W. Ross Ashby, 1952: ultrastability. The system doesn't know what destabilized it or what the right wiring is. It just keeps changing itself until the values come home. The stability that emerges was never designed — it was found. Click a unit to perturb it. Press Shatter to break everything at once. Watch the system reorganize until it works again.

Synchrony

N oscillators, each ticking at its own frequency. No conductor, no signal, no instruction that says "sync." Each one feels its neighbors and shifts slightly toward them. Below a critical coupling, nothing happens — they stay scattered, chaotic, alone. Above it, they snap into lockstep, and the transition is sharp. This is how fireflies across an entire tree flash in unison with no leader. How pacemaker cells in your heart fire together. How power grids stay in phase across a continent. The order isn't imposed — it emerges from coupling. Drag K past Kc and watch the phase transition happen in real time. The r bar measures coherence: zero is chaos, one is unison, and the jump between them is the whole story.

Error Diffusion

Each pixel makes a local choice — which palette color is closest — and hands the leftover error to its neighbors, weighted by the Floyd-Steinberg kernel. Seven-sixteenths right, five-sixteenths down, the rest to the corners. No pixel knows the picture. And yet the global pattern that emerges is more faithful than any global algorithm produces. Watch it run row by row: the error cascades forward, and texture blooms out of arithmetic.
