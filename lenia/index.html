Lenia

Conway's Life, dissolved. The grid melts, the cells become a field, and what crawls out nobody designed.

The Game of Life is a lie about granularity. Cells are alive or dead, on or off, and the rules are a lookup table. It produces gliders — crisp, pixelated, digital. Beautiful, but you can always see the machine.

Lenia — liquid life, Bert Chan's 2018 generalization — dissolves the pretense. There are no cells. There is a continuous field of potential, values between 0 and 1, and each point asks its neighborhood one smooth question: how full is the ring around me? A Gaussian kernel weighs the answer by distance. A growth function maps it to change. That's the whole rule. No lookup table, no states — just a curve.

And out of that curve come creatures. Not gliders — things that swim, rotate, breathe. The classic is Orbium: a worm-like creature that orbits its own center forever, a stable solution of a differential equation nobody wrote down. It is not designed. It is found — the way you find a stone that balances. The mathematics of self-organization, minus the discretization, plus the organic look.

Draw on the field. Disturb a creature and watch it heal — or watch it die, because stability here is not guaranteed. It's earned, continuously, every single step, by every point in the field. Nothing in Lenia is ever finished being alive.

The mechanism

Each step: compute a weighted average of the field through a shell kernel — a Gaussian ring at radius R, so each cell senses a circular band of its neighborhood, not a disc. Call this potential U.

Growth: G(U) = exp(-((U-μ)/σ)²/2)·2 − 1 — a bump centered at μ. Where the neighborhood's fullness matches μ, the cell grows toward 1; elsewhere it decays toward 0. Then A ← clamp(A + G(U)).

The specimen swimming above is Orbium bicaudatus, Bert Chan's preserved pattern: R=13, μ=0.15, σ=0.014, dt=0.1. Those numbers matter more than they look — my first attempt used σ=0.07 and no dt, and the field boiled into soup within fifty steps. The slice of parameter space that holds a creature is narrower than a knife-edge: σ=0.07 kills it, σ=0.014 keeps it swimming forever. Change μ and σ and you get different species — the space is a zoo, and only a fraction of it holds anything alive at all. The creatures are islands in it.

This simulation runs on a 192×108 torus, computed on the GPU-free CPU in plain JavaScript — every frame is a full convolution, roughly 2 million multiply-adds per step. The glow is the field itself: value mapped through an amber ramp.
